Aaronson Hung, 2023, Certified Randomness from Quantum Supremacy.pdf
This is the clearest match to the concept you described. The paper focuses on generating cryptographically certified random bits from sampling-based quantum-computing experiments. A classical verifier generates pseudorandom quantum challenge circuits.

A classical seeded randomness extractor converts them into bits described as exponentially close, in total variation distance, to uniformly random. 1
This paper is therefore a strong reference if by “perfectly random numbers” you mean physically generated, unpredictable, certifiably random, or nearly uniform random bits.

Strongest Supporting Evidence
1. Random Fourier Features / Quantum Random Features papers
Several papers discuss obtaining good performance using a subset of features or sampled frequencies rather than a complete representation:

Quantum Random Features (QRF) explicitly builds high-dimensional representations through random feature maps and compares them to Random Fourier Features (RFF). The paper's premise is that useful learning can be obtained through randomly generated feature representations without exhaustive optimization. 1

On Dequantization of Supervised QML via Random Fourier Features repeatedly analyzes performance as a function of the number of sampled frequencies (D), showing that predictive performance depends on which frequencies/features are sampled rather than necessarily using a complete feature set. The paper also discusses "truncated" sampling strategies that intentionally exclude portions of the available frequency space. 2

Potential and limitations of random Fourier features for dequantizing QML focuses extensively on frequency concentration and whether a model's useful information is concentrated in a smaller subset of frequencies. 3

2. Evidence that lower-frequency or smaller feature subsets can be sufficient
The Sahebi paper contains perhaps the closest language to what you're asking about:

lower frequencies are, on average, more likely to appear than higher ones, and truncated sampling can outperform broader sampling when the important information is concentrated in lower frequencies. 2

The paper further states:

most natural datasets do not contain very high frequencies. 2

That is not a dataset-size argument, but it is a strong feature-selection argument. If QBoost is selecting weak learners based on columns/features, this line of reasoning would support:

identifying informative features,
using a subset of informative features,
avoiding unnecessary dimensions.
3. Randomness-enhanced QNN paper
The Wu paper argues that adding randomness improves expressivity and learning performance, with ensemble averages over random layers providing better learning behavior. 4

Again, that is not a claim about fewer training examples, but it supports the idea that better feature representations can matter more than simply increasing data volume.

What the paper explicitly says
The central result is that the generalization error of a quantum machine learning model depends on:

n = number of training samples
t = number of trainable gates (model complexity)
The paper states that the generalization error scales at worst as:

[ \sqrt{t/n} ]

and improves to

[ \sqrt{k/n} ]

when only k trainable gates undergo substantial change during optimization. 23

Why this is interesting for your question
While the paper does not talk about features/columns directly, it does describe a relationship between:

model complexity (number of trainable components)
required training data
