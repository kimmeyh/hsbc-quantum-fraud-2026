# 1. Problem framing

Card fraud detection is not an offline accuracy contest. It is a decision made under two hard constraints: a fixed review capacity, because analysts can only work so many alerts a day, and an asymmetric cost, because a missed fraud costs a chargeback while a false positive costs a good customer. At the fraud rates of real portfolios, around 0.17% in the benchmark used here, a model that optimizes accuracy is worthless and a model that optimizes ranking is only useful if its ranking holds at the top of the list, where the review budget actually binds.

HSBC's problem statement asks whether quantum methods can improve detection in digital payment ecosystems. We took that question literally and built the apparatus to answer it in the form a bank could act on: a working classical detector, a quantum-inspired candidate evaluated against it under a protocol frozen before any result was seen, and a measured verdict reported in both directions.

This proposal states what we built, what we measured on real quantum hardware, what the measurement means, and what a 90-day production trial would run.

# 2. Technical approach

**A note on language.** The challenge title says quantum-enhanced; our results do not yet support that phrase for this arm, so we do not use it. What we present is a classical boosting formulation whose training step is solved on QCi's Dirac-3 entropy quantum computing hardware, and which did not beat the tuned classical detector on the primary endpoint. We reserve any claim of enhancement for a result measured against a strong or certified classical control.

**Paradigm**: hybrid classical plus quantum-inspired. The quantum component is CVQBoost on QCi's Dirac-3 entropy-computing optimizer, a continuous-variable quantum-inspired solver. A gate-based Amazon Braket arm is specified as a Phase 2 plan, not claimed as executed work.

**The system.** A tuned gradient-boosted ensemble (XGBoost, LightGBM, CatBoost, each given an equal 100-trial Optuna budget) is the production detector. CVQBoost is the evaluated candidate: it trains a pool of weak classifiers on small feature subsets, one depth-limited decision tree per feature and per correlation-ranked feature pair, each emitting a hard vote in {-1, +1} with no class weighting or subsampling in the frozen configuration, then selects ensemble weights by minimizing a quadratic objective on the simplex, which Dirac-3 solves natively. The method admits one-, two-, and three-feature subsets; every run reported here used one- and two-feature subsets, giving 91 weak learners over 13 features, because three-feature subsets would require 377 variables, beyond the Phase 1 allocation we had budgeted for pool construction and metered time. We note for precision that QCi's documented 949 limit is a sum over num_levels and applies to integer-encoded jobs; our runs are sum-constrained continuous jobs, to which it does not directly apply, so the binding constraint here was our own budget rather than a device ceiling. A third arm, the exact classical solve of the identical objective, serves as a structural control.

**The protocol is the contribution.** Before running anything we froze a preregistration: hypotheses, gates with numeric pass criteria, splits, seeds, metrics, statistical procedures, and the hardware budget. Changes since the freeze exist only as ten dated amendments, each with its rationale and approval, published in Appendix B. Concretely this means: gates are scored as committed even when they fail; every number carries an evidence tag ([HW] measured on hardware, [SIM] simulated or classical, [PROJ] projected); every number traces to a results record by configuration hash; leakage controls are enforced in code paths rather than by review; and a shuffled-label control confirms the pipeline collapses to chance when the signal is removed.

That discipline is why the results below can be trusted in the direction that is inconvenient for us.

# 3. What we measured

All figures: ULB benchmark, 284,807 transactions, 1,081 exact duplicates removed before splitting, 60/20/20 stratified, ten seeds, test-fold prevalence 0.00167. Full tables in Appendix A.

**Classical baselines are clean and strong.** CatBoost leads at 0.8368 AUPRC [SIM] (95% CI 0.8150 to 0.8585), with XGBoost and LightGBM within 0.013. The shuffled-label control collapses to 0.0023 against a base rate of 0.0017, and no cell approaches the 0.95 threshold that signals leakage.

**Gate G0 failed as committed.** Our preregistered floor of 0.85 AUPRC for tuned XGBoost was not met: 0.8296 [SIM]. We report it as a failure. Subsequent primary-source research found that the 0.85 to 0.88 band we had adopted is not corroborated: its probable origin computes an optimistic trapezoidal area, trains on 90% of the data with no held-out test fold, and retains duplicates. Clean-protocol equivalents fall near 0.80, and a benchmark using the same step-wise metric we use reports 0.78. We exceed both. The gate still reads FAIL, because moving a threshold after seeing the result is exactly what preregistration exists to prevent.

**Gate G0b passed on hardware.** Twenty-seven metered Dirac-3 fits, 120 QPU seconds, zero failures and zero retries. Ranking agreement between the classical proxy and hardware on validation AUPRC across the five preregistered configurations: Spearman 0.900 [HW]. We report the EXACT permutation p rather than the t-approximation scipy returns: 5 of the 120 possible rank orderings reach a coefficient of 0.900 or higher, so one-sided p = 0.042 and two-sided p = 0.083. Two qualifications we state rather than leave for a reader to find: n = 5 makes the interval wide, and the design deliberately includes two deliberately poor anchor configurations, one of which returns a constant score, which inflates a rank correlation. Restricted to the three competitive configurations the coefficient is 0.5, exactly at the gate; with three points Spearman can only take the values 1, 0.5, -0.5 and -1, so the smallest attainable one-sided p is about 0.167 and no restricted result could reach conventional significance. We report it as a descriptive check, not a test. The gate passes as committed, and we describe it for what it is: a smoke test that the proxy and the device rank configurations the same way, not strong validation of numerical agreement. Its role is to license proxy-based development, and it is sized accordingly.

**The primary hypothesis returned a null.** CVQBoost on Dirac-3 trails the best matched-feature GBDT by 0.0399 AUPRC [HW] (CI -0.0571 to -0.0227), beyond our minimum detectable effect of 0.0268, trailing on nine of ten seeds. The across-seed intervals we report measure split dispersion rather than sampling error, since the ten splits resample the same 283,726 rows and their test folds overlap; we label them accordingly. The direction does not depend on that interval: nine of ten seeds gives a two-sided sign test p of about 0.021 on its own. Six of ten per-seed paired bootstrap intervals exclude zero, all in the same direction [SIM]; those intervals are computed from proxy scores, for the reason given below.

**Why, precisely, and the control that settles it.** The CVQBoost objective is a convex quadratic on the simplex: the Gram matrix of weak-learner outputs is positive semidefinite by construction and the penalty term is positive, so the optimum is unique and reachable in polynomial time. That is a theorem, not a measurement, and we state it before measuring: our hardware-versus-proxy agreement is a confirmation of it rather than a discovery.

The stronger finding is what the optimum turns out to be. **It is exactly uniform.** Across all ten seeds the solved weight vector sits at 1/91 on every learner, with an L1 distance from uniform of 0.000000 [SIM]. We therefore ran the control that this demands: scoring the same pool with uniform weights by construction gives 0.7659 AUPRC against the solved 0.7681, a difference of 0.0022, an order of magnitude below our own minimum detectable effect. **On this configuration the optimization step contributes nothing measurable, and neither a quantum nor a classical solver is doing useful work.**

The cause is measurable and is neither the constraint geometry nor class imbalance in the loss. The weak learners are near-identical: off-diagonal Gram entries average 170,234.4 against a diagonal of 170,235, so any two learners agree on 99.999% of training rows. At 0.17% prevalence a depth-limited tree predicts the negative class almost everywhere, so the pool carries almost no diversity for a weighting to exploit. A class-weighted re-solve, weighting fraud rows by inverse prevalence, moves the optimum by an L1 distance of 0.002 and leaves it uniform, which rules out the loss weighting as the explanation. We measured this rather than asserting it: hardware minus exact proxy is -0.0010 AUPRC [HW] with the interval containing zero, solution weights agree to cosine 0.975 to 0.999, and hardware objective values are always above the exact minimum, never below, by margins of 0.013% to 0.413% of the objective value. Dirac-3 is a stochastic solver, so each fit is a draw rather than a deterministic answer; every fit requested eight samples and no fit returned identical energies across them, yet the within-fit spread has a median of 0.019% and a maximum of 0.343% [HW]. The device is measurably stochastic and its dispersion is small, which is the same conclusion from another direction: near this optimum the surface is flat enough that sampling variation barely moves the answer. Nor is there a speed argument at this size: a Dirac-3 fit bills 4 to 5 seconds, while the exact classical solve of the same 91-variable convex problem completes in milliseconds on a laptop. A regularization sweep confirms the same conclusion from another direction: the optimum stays uniform even at zero penalty, so the penalty term is not the cause either. On this formulation the optimization problem is degenerate, which is why any solver reproduces it.

The regularization sweep is an exploratory analysis, not the preregistered structural control: the two committed controls, a tuned-penalty non-negative ridge and a sparse variant, have not been run, so the structural-attribution hypothesis is reported as partial rather than scored.

This is a negative result about one formulation, not about the approach, and it relocates the Phase 2 question. The first requirement is not a better solver but a pool with genuine diversity, since no optimizer can weight interchangeable learners usefully. That points at deeper or differently-seeded weak learners, feature subsets chosen for decorrelation, and the sparse-selection formulation in section 6, which is the one that would force the optimizer to choose rather than average.

**Two facts about the selected configuration that a bank would want stated together.** It carries a score-degeneracy warning on all ten seeds: 95.1% of transactions share one score across 814 distinct values, so its ranking metrics are sound but its threshold-dependent figures are weak evidence. And on the single time-ordered split it falls to 0.7095 while the 9-feature configuration we did not select rises to 0.7776 [HW], above the selected configuration's own random-split result. Selection followed the preregistered validation-only rule and we did not revisit it after seeing test results. On the evidence as it stands, the healthy 9-feature configuration is the one we would carry into a trial, subject to the temporal test being run properly.

**In operational terms the gap is smaller than the metric suggests.** At a 0.1% review budget the production detector, all-feature CatBoost, catches 59.3% of test-fold fraud; the CVQBoost arm catches 56.5% [SIM], a 2.8 point difference. Against the matched-13 CatBoost that H1b uses as its comparator the figure is 58.0%. One caveat belongs in the same breath: that budget funds 57 alerts against about 95 frauds, so attainable recall is capped at 0.600 and CatBoost reaches 98.8% of the ceiling. The narrow gap is therefore partly budget saturation, not only model quality. The 0.5% budget is the only unsaturated column we have, and there the figures are 85.5% and 81.9%. At a 0.5% budget the figures are 85.5% and 81.9%, a 3.6 point difference. These operating points are computed from the exact classical proxy, not from hardware: Dirac-3 solution weights were not persisted during the Sprint 4 campaign, so per-transaction hardware scores cannot be recovered without spending further metered time. The substitution is licensed by measurement rather than assumption, since hardware and proxy differ by -0.0010 AUPRC with the interval containing zero, but the tag is [SIM] and stays [SIM] until a hardware run persists its predictions.

# 4. Production trial and expected impact

The apparatus above is what a bank would need before piloting anything, so we specify the pilot rather than gesture at it.

**A 90-day shadow-mode trial.** Days 1-30: the tuned classical ensemble scores live traffic in shadow, deciding nothing, while its alerts are compared against the incumbent's on the same transactions. The comparison metric is the one the fraud team already uses, fraud caught within the standing review budget, not AUPRC. Days 31-60: champion/challenger, with the challenger taking a small routed share of decisions under a rollback trigger tied to precision at the alert budget. Days 61-90: the quantum-inspired arm enters the same shadow track as a specialist candidate, evaluated on the segments identified in Phase 2 rather than on aggregate performance.

**What the trial must satisfy to proceed.** Latency: real-time scoring budgets are in the low tens of milliseconds, which the classical ensemble meets and which the CVQBoost arm meets trivially at inference, since inference is a weighted vote over small classifiers; the quantum solve happens at training time only. Calibration: the downstream rules engine consumes a score, so the model ships with a fitted calibration layer and reliability diagrams, reported here with equal-mass binning. Drift: fraud patterns decay in weeks, so the trial defines retraining triggers on population stability and on precision-at-budget degradation rather than on a fixed calendar. Our own temporal check is a warning worth naming precisely. On a single time-ordered split the two configurations swapped places, and the benchmark spans only two days, so this tests intra-day ordering rather than weeks-scale concept drift: the configuration we selected fell from 0.7671 to 0.7095 AUPRC, while the configuration we rejected rose from 0.7014 to 0.7776 [HW]. One split proves nothing and we claim nothing from it, but it points the wrong way for our own choice, and establishing which configuration survives time ordering is the first thing the trial should measure.

**Explainability, which is the bar that actually gates adoption.** A CVQBoost ensemble is a weighted vote over classifiers that each read one to three named features, so a decision decomposes into exact arithmetic terms rather than a post-hoc approximation. We measured this on a real flagged fraud: the score is the sum of per-learner contributions, each naming its feature pair and its weight. The honest counterpart, also measured: at the current configuration weight is spread widely, taking 46 of 91 learners to reach half the total, so the explanation is exact but long. A sparser formulation would shorten it, which is one more reason it is the Phase 2 direction.

**Two requirements we have not yet met, stated rather than omitted.** Fairness and disparate-impact testing across protected characteristics is a regulatory expectation in UK retail banking, under the Equality Act 2010 and FCA Consumer Duty, and is absent from this work; the benchmark's anonymized features make it untestable here, and it becomes a first-class requirement the moment real customer data is in scope. Label latency is the second: chargeback labels arrive weeks after the transaction, so the trial's evaluation window must account for censored labels rather than treating recent unlabeled transactions as legitimate.

**Expected impact, quantified.** Under the trial's own metric the decision is whether a candidate arm raises fraud caught within a fixed analyst budget, so the impact of any recall difference is arithmetic. For a portfolio processing one million transactions a day at the benchmark's 0.17% fraud rate, a 0.1% review budget funds 1,000 alerts against roughly 1,700 frauds, which caps attainable recall at 58.8% before any model is chosen; within that ceiling each percentage point of recall is about 17 additional frauds caught per day [PROJ]. The 2.8 point gap we measure between the production detector and the current quantum-inspired arm is therefore roughly 48 frauds a day at that scale, which is the number a trial would have to move. We give this as an illustration of the arithmetic, not a forecast: it assumes a portfolio we have not seen, and the benchmark spans two days.

Our current measurement says the classical ensemble is the detector to deploy today, the quantum-inspired arm is within three percentage points of caught fraud without yet having its best formulation, and the routing architecture that would combine them is specified rather than speculative.

**The first thing Phase 2 should resolve** is the temporal split reversal described in section 3. The configuration our preregistered rule selected degrades under time ordering while the one it rejected improves, and fraud models are deployed into a moving distribution. Until that is settled with a proper temporal protocol rather than one split, no configuration of this arm should be recommended for a production decision path.

# 5. Hybrid integration

The deployed shape is a classical detector with a quantum-inspired specialist behind a router. The classical ensemble scores every transaction. The specialist is invoked on the segments where it is measured to help, at training-time cost only, since its inference is a linear vote. The quantum hardware sits at training and model-selection time, where its solve is a single call of a few seconds, not in the transaction path. This division is why the hybrid is deployable at all: nothing in the live path depends on a quantum device being reachable.

# 6. Phase 2 program

Four levers remain open, each with a preregistered test already written.

**Representation.** Quantum feature engineering, applied identically to every arm with order-matched classical twins, tests whether a phase-based representation shifts the quantum-minus-classical difference. The twins matter: omitting them is how apparent quantum wins are manufactured.

**Segment specialization.** The team lead's prior Dirac-3 campaign found in-segment wins for this model family on non-fraud benchmarks (single seed, small margins; recorded in the project's evidence inventory and reproduced in the public package); the preregistered test replicates them with seeds and confidence intervals, then tests transfer to fraud segments defined a priori, with a matched random-segment control.

**Scale and sparsity.** The IEEE-CIS regime, with 590,540 transactions and several hundred features, is where the model form has room to differ, and it is where the feature-ladder dose-response test lives.

**Training time against sample count, which is the vendor's actual claim.** CVQBoost's published claim is a runtime advantage at competitive accuracy, tested on synthetic sets from one million to seventy million samples, and QCi's own fraud tutorial reports comparable AUC for QBoost and XGBoost on a balanced subsample. Our accuracy null at 284,000 rows is therefore consistent with the vendor's own reporting rather than contrary to it: accuracy was never the claim being made. The sharper first experiment is the one we have not run, end-to-end training time against sample count including Hamiltonian construction, whose classical cost grows with the square of the pool size and is the term that decides whether the scaling claim survives.

**The formulation where quantum optimization is necessary.** Our convexity result is the strongest argument for the next experiment: the continuous relaxation is classically solvable, so the interesting problem is the combinatorial one it relaxes. Choosing the best sparse subset of weak learners under a cardinality constraint is NP-hard in general and has no polynomial-time exact proxy, and it is the native problem class for Dirac-3's integer solver. At the sizes we would test first, a commercial mixed-integer solver may still certify a global optimum, so the preregistered controls for that experiment are a time-capped MIQP solve alongside greedy and annealing heuristics. A win against a certified-optimal classical solve is a result; a win against no control is not. That is the Phase 2 experiment we would run first, and the reason hardware access matters to it.

We also disclose a constraint honestly: the 13-feature configurations used here were sized to a free-tier variable limit, not to the problem. Production sizing is the reverse, and the corrected variable arithmetic in Appendix B gives the bounds.
