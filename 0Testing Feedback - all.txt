  Decision 1: Should we run three extra checks on the classical results?

  The situation. We set a target before starting: our standard model had to score at least 0.85. It scored 0.8296. That's a miss, and it stays
  recorded as a miss — we don't move goalposts after seeing results.

  But we later dug into where the 0.85 came from. It traces back to a tutorial that did three things differently than we did, and all three
  make scores look higher: it measured the score with a method that rounds in its own favor, it trained on almost all the data with nothing
  held back for a fair final test, and it kept duplicate rows that are easy to guess. Redone properly, that source's number is closer to 0.80.
  Another study that measured the honest way got 0.78. We got 0.8296 — better than both.

  The proposal (called A7). Run three extra checks to prove that explanation instead of just asserting it:
  - Re-measure our existing results using the optimistic method, to see how much of the gap that alone explains.
  - Re-run with duplicates left in, to measure what they're worth. Nobody has ever published this number for this dataset.
  - Re-run with a bigger training slice, to measure what the extra data is worth.

  Cost: no quantum time, a few hours of computer time overnight. The original target stays failed either way.

  My recommendation: approve. It turns a footnote into measured evidence, and the duplicate number would be a small original contribution.

  ---

  Decision 2: What story does the paper tell?

  The situation. Here's what we actually found. The best regular model scores 0.837. The quantum method scores 0.767 on real quantum hardware —
  it loses, and it loses on 9 of the 10 tests we ran, so it's a real gap, not luck.

  We also found why, and this is the interesting part. The quantum method's math problem has one single best answer that an ordinary computer
  can find exactly. We proved that by solving it both ways: the quantum machine and the ordinary computer landed in the same place (difference:
  0.001, which is nothing). So on this particular problem, the quantum machine has nothing to add — not because it's weak, but because the
  problem is too easy in a specific mathematical sense.

  Three ways to write it up:
  1. The honest map. "We tested carefully where this quantum method helps and where it doesn't, and here's the map." True right now. Note: our
     own rules said we'd fall back to this only after three tests failed, and two of them haven't been run yet — so we'd be choosing this
     early, and we should say so.
  2. The practical system. "The best fraud detector today is the classical one; the quantum piece has a specific job in specific places, and
     here's the plan to prove it." Matches your goal of best predictions regardless of source.
  3. Quantum wins. Not honest with these numbers.

  My recommendation: option 1 as the backbone, option 2 as the pitch. The map plus the proof of why is more convincing to a reviewer than
  another chart claiming a win, because most submissions can't explain their own results that precisely.

  ---

  Decision 3: Should we add a Phase 2 research item?

  The situation. We now know the current quantum setup asks the machine an easy question. There's a harder version of the same question — "pick
  the best 20 out of 91 helpers" instead of "assign weights to all 91" — and that harder version is genuinely difficult for ordinary
  computers. That's the kind of problem quantum optimization is actually built for.

  The proposal: register it as a future item (F25) so it's on the record. Nothing runs now.

  My recommendation: approve. It gives the paper a concrete, credible "here's what we do next and why quantum is necessary for it" — which is
  exactly what a judge funding a Phase 2 wants to see, and it's the strongest thing to put in front of QCi.
